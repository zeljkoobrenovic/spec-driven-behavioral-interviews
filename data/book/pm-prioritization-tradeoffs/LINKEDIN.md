Product prioritization is not tested by whether a PM says they "used RICE."

Frameworks are only decision aids.

The real signal is whether they can choose among attractive opportunities by making customer value, business impact, technical feasibility, confidence, risk, and opportunity cost explicit.

When I interview PMs about prioritization and trade-offs, I listen for five things:

- Can they compare real opportunities, not a strong option against a straw man?
- Can they explain the evidence behind the decision criteria instead of hiding behind framework scores?
- Can they name what was delayed, reduced, stopped, or deliberately not built?
- Can they show how Engineering, Design, Sales, Support, and customer evidence changed the product judgment?
- Can they define the revisit trigger, guardrail, or portfolio mechanism that prevents the same debate from reopening every week?

Weak prioritization stories often sound reasonable at first:

"We scored the options by impact, confidence, and effort, then picked the highest score."

"Sales wanted one thing and users wanted another, so I aligned everyone around the roadmap."

"We put the lower-priority work in the backlog and focused on the highest-value opportunity."

None of these are automatically bad. They are just not enough for product leadership.

At Product Manager level, I want to hear how the person compared opportunities with concrete evidence, named the trade-off, communicated the decision, and created a bounded mitigation for the deferred work.

At Senior Product Manager level, I want to hear how they balanced customer, business, UX, technical, and execution constraints across a larger scope, and how they distinguished a repeated segment signal from a loud one-off request.

At Group Product Manager level, I want to hear how they managed opportunity cost across product areas or teams, rebalanced capacity when evidence changed, and created explicit stop-doing rules.

The most useful follow-ups are concrete:

- What criteria actually decided the priority?
- What did you explicitly choose not to do?
- Who disagreed with your priority, and what were they right about?
- How did engineering input change the product decision?
- What evidence would have made you reverse or rebalance the decision?
- What made this a stop or shrink decision rather than a normal delay?
- How did this priority affect other teams, bets, or capacity?
- What changed in the prioritization process afterward?

The strongest answers rarely sound like "I prioritized the highest-impact feature."

They sound more like:

"We had to choose between improving self-serve activation and building an enterprise admin workflow requested by three large accounts. Both were real opportunities. Activation drop-off affected 42 percent of trials and blocked the company's self-serve strategy, but Sales was right that one renewal could become risky if the admin work disappeared without mitigation. I compared segment size, expected revenue impact, funnel data, effort, technical risk, and renewal exposure. We chose activation, deferred the full admin workflow, and created a customer-success playbook with a renewal-risk trigger for reopening the decision. I published the lost enterprise upside, named the owner for the residual renewal risk, and set the activation metric target. When one account escalated later, we kept the activation bet but added a narrow admin-export spike to test whether the deferred risk was growing."

That answer shows product judgment, not just roadmap order.

It names the attractive option that lost.

It treats Sales input as evidence to test, not noise to dismiss.

It includes engineering and execution risk.

It owns residual cost.

It defines when the team should revisit the call.

Senior product prioritization is not about ranking a backlog.

It is about making trade-offs visible enough that stakeholders can understand them, challenge them, and trust the mechanism even when their preferred bet loses.
