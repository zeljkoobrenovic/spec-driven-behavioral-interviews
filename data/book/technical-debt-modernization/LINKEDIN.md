Technical debt leadership is not tested by whether someone says they "care about code quality."

Code quality is table stakes.

The real signal is whether they can translate debt into business risk, prioritize what matters, and fund modernization without pretending the roadmap can simply stop.

When I interview senior technology leaders about technical debt and modernization, I listen for five things:

- Can they connect debt to customer impact, revenue delay, reliability, cloud cost, on-call load, hiring friction, or delivery capacity?
- Can they separate business-impacting debt from irritating or aesthetic debt?
- Can they explain what they deliberately chose not to fix, who owned that risk, and what trigger would reopen the decision?
- Can they sequence modernization through product milestones instead of asking for an isolated cleanup quarter?
- Can they leave behind a governance mechanism that keeps debt visible after the project ends?

Weak technical debt stories often sound reasonable at first:

"The system was old and slowing engineers down, so I asked for a quarter to rebuild it properly."

"We fixed the ugliest parts first because the team had been complaining about them for years."

"I reserved capacity for cleanup while keeping the product roadmap unchanged."

None of these are automatically bad. They are just not enough for senior leadership.

At Senior Manager level, I want to hear how the person protected a high-risk migration slice while managing delivery, reliability, on-call load, and team capacity.

At Director level, I want to hear how they sequenced modernization across teams, dependencies, product commitments, and stakeholder expectations.

At VP Engineering level, I want to hear how they created a portfolio view of technical risk and made explicit stop-doing choices in roadmap planning.

At CTO level, I want to hear how they connected modernization to company strategy, operating leverage, risk posture, and capital allocation, including the debt the company would intentionally carry.

The most useful follow-ups are concrete:

- How did you make the business case?
- Which debt did you choose not to address?
- How did you fund the work without freezing product delivery?
- What customer, cost, reliability, or team-capacity metric changed?
- Who disagreed with the scope, and what alternative did you reject?
- How did the debt stay visible after the project?
- What risk did you accept, and when would you revisit it?

The strongest answers rarely sound like "I convinced the business to clean up the platform."

They sound more like:

"Legacy provisioning was delaying enterprise onboarding, creating support escalations, and putting about $3M of pipeline at risk. We compared a full rewrite, one-off sales scripts, targeted migration, and deferral. I rejected the rewrite and the scripts, funded migration slices through enterprise roadmap milestones, traded off two lower-value admin features, and tracked onboarding time from 42 days to 12 days with provisioning incidents down roughly one-third. We left a low-volume path alone with an owner, review date, and trigger metric."

Senior technical debt leadership is not engineering purity.

It is economic framing, prioritization, product trade-off discipline, residual-risk ownership, and an operating mechanism that makes modernization a business decision rather than a recurring plea for cleanup time.
