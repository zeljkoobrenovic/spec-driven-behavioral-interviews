Executive communication is not tested by whether someone says they "simplified the technical details."

Simplification is table stakes.

The real signal is whether they can help executives, business partners, or the board make a good decision when the technical reality is complex, uncertain, and consequential.

When I interview senior technology leaders about executive communication, I listen for four things:

- Can they start with the decision, recommendation, and owner rather than the implementation story?
- Can they translate technical issues into customer, financial, delivery, legal, security, or trust consequences?
- Can they separate facts, assumptions, unknowns, confidence levels, and decision thresholds?
- Can they present comparable options with trade-offs, rejected alternatives, residual risk, and next actions?

Weak executive communication stories often sound reasonable at first:

"I explained the architecture clearly so the board understood why the migration was delayed."

"I told the executive team we were still investigating and would come back when we had the full picture."

"I simplified the data pipeline issue for Customer Success and Finance and asked them to wait for Engineering to fix it."

None of these are automatically bad. They are just not enough for senior leadership.

At Senior Manager level, I want to hear how the person turned a team-level technical risk into a business decision with practical options and ownership of the cost.

At Director level, I want to hear how they communicated cross-team risk with clear decision criteria, customer consequences, and rejected alternatives.

At VP Engineering level, I want to hear how they framed organizational technology risk for executives with scenarios, confidence levels, cost, timing, and a recommendation.

At CTO level, I want to hear how they communicated company-level technical uncertainty to the CEO, board, customers, or external stakeholders while balancing trust, legal exposure, financial impact, and timing.

The most useful follow-ups are concrete:

- What decision did the audience need to make?
- What did you recommend, and what alternatives did you reject?
- What did you know, what did you assume, and what was still unknown?
- How did you translate the technical issue into business or customer consequences?
- What trade-off did your recommendation create, and who absorbed the cost?
- How did executives push back?
- What communication format, risk brief, decision template, or cadence changed afterward?

The strongest answers rarely sound like "I made it easy to understand."

They sound more like:

"The board did not need an architecture lesson. They needed to choose between holding the migration date with higher customer risk, funding a parallel reliability track, or accepting a staged migration. I separated confirmed failure modes, assumptions, unknowns, and confidence levels; compared the options by customer impact, cash cost, engineering effort, timing, and residual risk; recommended the staged path; and created a one-page technical risk brief for future escalations."

Senior executive communication is not translation.

It is decision design: making uncertainty usable, trade-offs visible, recommendations accountable, and the next operating mechanism better than the last one.
