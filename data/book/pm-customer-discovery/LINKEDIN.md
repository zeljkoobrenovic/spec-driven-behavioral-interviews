Customer discovery is not tested by whether a PM says they "talked to customers."

Talking to customers is table stakes.

The real signal is whether they can turn customer evidence into a clearer problem, a focused product direction, and a measurable learning loop.

When I interview PMs about customer discovery and product direction, I listen for five things:

- Can they distinguish customer requests from the underlying job, workflow, or pain?
- Can they use more than one evidence type: interviews, support tickets, usage data, revenue context, observed behavior, or churn notes?
- Can they name the segment they optimized for and the attractive segment or feature path they did not optimize for?
- Can they explain how discovery changed, confirmed, or constrained the product direction?
- Can they define the behavior metric, checkpoint, or kill criterion that would tell them whether the decision worked?

Weak discovery stories often sound reasonable at first:

"Customers asked for the feature, so I gathered requirements and prioritized it."

"We interviewed users and validated that there was demand."

"Discovery showed we needed to pivot, so we changed the roadmap and got stakeholders aligned."

None of these are automatically bad. They are just not enough for product judgment.

At Product Manager level, I want to hear how the person clarified the customer problem from the request, scoped a focused solution, and measured behavior change.

At Senior Product Manager level, I want to hear how they handled ambiguous evidence across segments, changed or constrained direction, and aligned stakeholders around a learning plan.

At Group Product Manager level, I want to hear how they coordinated discovery across personas or product areas, made segment trade-offs explicit, and created a reusable decision mechanism.

The most useful follow-ups are concrete:

- What evidence changed or constrained your product direction?
- Which customers did you choose not to optimize for first?
- What was the customer job behind the feature request?
- What broader version did you reject?
- When did discovery confirm the original request rather than overturn it?
- What metric told you customer behavior had changed?
- What checkpoint or kill criterion did you set?
- What changed in your discovery or roadmap process afterward?

The strongest answers rarely sound like "I listened to customers and built what they needed."

They sound more like:

"Enterprise customers kept asking for bulk invoice export. I initially treated it as a reporting request, but eight customer calls, support tickets, and account-level usage showed the real problem was reconciling billing exceptions across child accounts. A generic export would have moved the work into spreadsheets without solving the workflow. We chose enterprise admins as the first segment, rejected a broad reporting suite, and shipped exception review, filtered export, and an audit trail. Success was not export clicks; it was fewer reconciliation support tickets and lower task completion time. Sales was disappointed we were not building the broader reporting ask, so I gave them a clear segment decision, residual risk, and revisit trigger."

Senior customer discovery is not about proving that a PM is customer-focused.

It is about evidence quality, segment judgment, scope discipline, stakeholder honesty, and the ability to turn learning into a better product decision than the one the team started with.
