Culture change is not tested by whether someone says they "changed the mindset."

Mindset is not the mechanism.

The real signal is whether they can diagnose repeated behavior under pressure, change what leaders reward, and keep the new behavior alive when delivery pressure comes back.

When I interview senior technology leaders about culture change, I listen for four things:

- Can they describe the culture as observable behavior, not abstract values?
- Can they explain what incentives, rituals, decision rights, or leadership habits reinforced the old behavior?
- Can they preserve the useful part of the old culture while removing the part that was causing damage?
- Can they show that the new behavior survived a pressure test, not just an all-hands announcement?

Weak culture-change stories often sound reasonable at first:

"I told the organization we needed to be more proactive."

"I stopped praising heroics and asked managers to recognize prevention."

"I reminded teams that postmortems should be blameless."

"I asked the founder to delegate more and encouraged teams to own decisions."

None of these are automatically bad. They are just not enough for senior leadership.

At Senior Manager level, I want to hear how the person changed team norms through explicit expectations, feedback, and repeatable rituals.

At Director level, I want to hear how they changed cross-team rituals, decision rights, and incentives that shaped daily engineering behavior.

At VP Engineering level, I want to hear how they changed leadership expectations, operating cadence, recognition systems, and accountability mechanisms across the organization.

At CTO level, I want to hear how they shaped company technology culture in service of strategy, trust, and sustainable execution.

The most useful follow-ups are concrete:

- What specific behavior told you the culture needed to change?
- What did leaders stop rewarding?
- Which part of the old culture did you intentionally preserve?
- What incentive, ritual, decision right, or leadership behavior changed?
- How did you personally model the new behavior?
- What resistance or early miss changed your approach?
- What pressure test showed the change had sustained?

The strongest answers rarely sound like "I changed the culture."

They sound more like:

"We had a hero culture that celebrated last-minute launch saves while prevention, documentation, and operational excellence were invisible. I separated true customer emergencies from avoidable rescue work, stopped treating preventable saves as promotion evidence, added operational excellence to planning and launch readiness, changed recognition toward durable fixes, and coached leaders to name their own misses in incident reviews. The first readiness checklist was too heavy, so we simplified it after teams started bypassing it. In the next peak launch window, teams still escalated real customer risk quickly, but routine launches no longer depended on after-hours rescues; repeat incidents and reopened action items dropped."

Senior culture change is not messaging.

It is behavioral diagnosis, incentive design, leadership modeling, pressure-tested learning, and a new operating mechanism that makes the desired behavior easier to repeat than the old one.
