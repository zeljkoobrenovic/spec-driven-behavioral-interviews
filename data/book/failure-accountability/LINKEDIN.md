Failure and accountability is not tested by whether someone says they "owned it."

Ownership is table stakes.

The real signal is whether they can name the leadership miss, communicate customer or business impact while facts are still incomplete, and change the operating system that allowed the failure to happen.

When I interview senior technology leaders about failure, I listen for four things:

- Can they name what they personally approved, missed, underestimated, or failed to escalate?
- Can they explain who was affected without hiding behind technical detail or blaming another team?
- Can they communicate facts, uncertainty, next-update timing, and recovery options before root cause is fully known?
- Can they turn the failure into a durable control, decision habit, rollout mechanism, or governance change?

Weak failure stories often sound reasonable at first:

"The migration had unexpected issues, the team worked around the clock, and afterward we improved testing."

"A dependency team slipped and there was not much we could do, but we adjusted the timeline and communicated the delay."

"A script had an edge-case bug, we restored the data from backup, and I reminded the team to test bulk jobs more carefully."

None of these are automatically bad. They are just not enough for senior leadership.

At Senior Manager level, I want to hear which shortcut they approved, how they contained the customer or team impact, and what release control changed afterward.

At Director level, I want to hear how they owned a cross-team miss and changed dependency, readiness, or escalation mechanisms.

At VP Engineering level, I want to hear how they handled executives, customer-facing teams, mixed outcomes, and organizational trade-offs without polishing the story into a clean win.

At CTO level, I want to hear how they owned company-level technology trust and changed governance, risk posture, and external communication.

The most useful follow-ups are concrete:

- What did you personally miss?
- What did you tell customers or executives before root cause was clear?
- Which metric improved while another outcome got worse?
- What residual cost remained after recovery?
- What rollout gate, readiness review, escalation path, or approval mechanism changed afterward?
- How did you verify that the same leadership failure would be less likely next time?

The strongest answers rarely sound like "we learned a lot."

They sound more like:

"I treated executive alignment as readiness even though we did not have production-like evidence. When the migration created customer-impacting instability, I separated incident command from executive and customer communication, paused the rollout, published known impact and next-update times before root cause was complete, and accepted that the quarter's migration goal had to move. Afterward, we added migration readiness reviews with evidence requirements, progressive rollout stop conditions, named rollback owners, and executive-visible tracking for post-incident actions."

Senior accountability is not public self-criticism.

It is specific ownership, honest impact communication, pressure-tested recovery, and a changed operating mechanism that makes the same failure harder to repeat.
