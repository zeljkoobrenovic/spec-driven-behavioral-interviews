Coaching engineers is not tested by whether someone says they are "supportive."

Being available is table stakes.

The real signal is whether an engineering manager can diagnose the growth need, give direct feedback, create a bounded opportunity, measure evidence, and know when coaching needs to become a firmer boundary.

When I interview engineering managers about coaching and developing engineers, I listen for five things:

- Can they describe the gap in observable behavior, not personality labels?
- Can they give feedback that is both direct and usable?
- Can they create a real growth opportunity without putting the team at unmanaged risk?
- Can they measure progress against role expectations, not confidence, effort, or visibility?
- Can they distinguish a teachable development gap from repeated behavior that now requires scope change, role refocus, or formal performance management?

Weak coaching stories often sound reasonable at first:

"I encouraged them to take more ownership."

"I gave them a stretch project so they could prove themselves."

"They were talented, so I kept coaching them until they were ready."

None of these are automatically bad. They are just not enough for engineering leadership.

At Engineering Manager level, I want to hear how the person diagnosed the need, gave actionable feedback, designed support, protected the team, and tracked evidence.

At Senior Engineering Manager level, I want to hear how they improved coaching quality across managers, repaired overpromised promotion narratives, calibrated next-level evidence, and created repeatable development mechanisms.

The most useful follow-ups are concrete:

- How did you diagnose what the engineer actually needed?
- What difficult feedback did you give, and how did you make it actionable?
- How did you choose the growth opportunity without setting them up to fail?
- What evidence showed the growth was real?
- What feedback or boundary did the engineer initially resist?
- What did not improve?
- When would coaching have stopped being enough?
- What mechanism changed afterward so development was less dependent on your personal judgment?

The strongest answers rarely sound like "I helped them grow."

They sound more like:

"A strong backend engineer wanted promotion to senior level. Their code quality was high, but they waited for me to coordinate dependencies, surface delivery risk, and unblock peers. I told them directly that senior-level evidence required owning a project outcome, not only difficult implementation tasks. I did not promote on potential, and I did not put them on a critical launch as a prove-yourself test. I gave them a bounded reporting API with two dependencies, a planning template, weekly risk review, and examples of strong stakeholder updates. The first risk update was late, so I coached it without silently taking the work back. After two cycles, they were surfacing risks earlier, closing dependencies without me, and getting peer feedback as a credible project owner. We promoted after the evidence was real, and I changed our project-lead readiness checklist so future coaching covered planning, risk, communication, and delegation earlier."

That answer shows diagnosis, directness, opportunity design, resistance, evidence, promotion calibration, and a durable management mechanism.

Engineering coaching is not generic encouragement.

It is clear expectations, tailored support, honest feedback, measured growth, explicit boundaries, and a better system after the story.
