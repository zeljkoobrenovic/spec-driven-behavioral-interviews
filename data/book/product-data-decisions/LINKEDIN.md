Data-informed product decision-making is not tested by whether a PM says they "looked at the dashboard."

Dashboards are only evidence.

The real signal is whether they can explain what the metric measured, what it missed, which customer behavior mattered, and why the decision changed, narrowed, or held despite pressure.

When I interview PMs about data-informed product decisions, I listen for five things:

- Can they explain what made the data trustworthy, misleading, noisy, biased, or invalid?
- Can they connect metric movement to customer behavior rather than treating usage, engagement, or conversion as automatically good?
- Can they combine quantitative data with cohort behavior, customer feedback, support signals, and business context?
- Can they name the decision they changed, narrowed, stopped, or defended because of the evidence?
- Can they show what changed afterward in dashboards, experiment readouts, guardrails, or decision rules?

Weak data stories often sound reasonable at first:

"The data changed our mind, so we pivoted."

"Engagement went up, so we rolled out the feature."

"The dashboard looked good, but customers complained, so we kept iterating."

"The experiment was invalid because tracking had issues, so we fixed analytics."

None of these are automatically bad. They are just not enough for senior product judgment.

A strong answer does not use data as decoration.

It shows how the candidate judged evidence quality, interpreted behavior, owned the trade-off, and improved the measurement system after the decision.

At Senior Product Manager level, I want to hear how the person used data to change, refine, or defend a real product decision, checked metric limits, and defined a better success measure.

At Group Product Manager level, I want to hear how they helped multiple PMs interpret evidence, set guardrails, check instrumentation quality, and avoid declaring local metric wins too early.

At Director of Product level, I want to hear how they connected product metrics to customer segments, portfolio investment, business outcomes, and durable measurement governance.

The most useful follow-ups are concrete:

- What made the metric trustworthy or misleading?
- What did the metric fail to measure?
- Which segment, cohort, denominator, or time horizon changed your interpretation?
- What decision did you change, narrow, stop, or hold because of the data?
- Who wanted the favorable readout preserved?
- What short-term number got worse because you made the better decision?
- When did customer feedback represent the target segment, and when was it valid but not representative?
- What changed in the experiment readout, dashboard, guardrail, or decision process afterward?

The strongest answers rarely sound like "I used the data to make the right call."

They sound more like:

"We launched a recommendations feature that lifted weekly active use by 11 percent, and Growth wanted to expand it. The first readout looked like a win, but support tickets, cohort analysis, and customer sessions showed users were reopening recommendations because they could not find the right next action. Engagement was real, but it was measuring repeated confusion, not value. I paused the rollout even though it put our quarterly adoption commitment at risk. We changed the success metric from recommendation opens to completed next action and time-to-decision, shipped a narrower redesign, and added a guardrail for repeated low-value engagement before future recommendation experiments could scale."

That answer shows product judgment.

It explains why the favorable metric was misleading.

It connects the metric to observed customer behavior.

It names the stakeholder pressure and short-term cost.

It changes the decision and the measurement mechanism.

It avoids both forms of bad product data thinking: metric obedience and anecdote obedience.

Senior product leaders do not need to distrust dashboards.

They need to know what the dashboard is actually evidence of.

The best product data stories are not about being "data-driven."

They are about being accountable for the decision the data informs.
