Handling underperformance is not tested by whether someone says they had a "tough conversation."

Tough conversations are table stakes.

The real signal is whether they can separate a skill gap, role mismatch, motivation problem, and harmful behavior, then act with clarity, fairness, compassion, and timing.

When I interview senior technology leaders about underperformance, I listen for four things:

- Can they name the specific expectation the person failed to meet?
- Can they use evidence instead of reputation, frustration, or personality labels?
- Can they match the intervention to the failure mode: coaching for a teachable gap, scope change for role mismatch, and faster boundaries for harmful behavior?
- Can they protect the team while treating the person with dignity?

Weak underperformance stories often sound reasonable at first:

"They were a good person, but they just were not scaling."

"I gave them feedback for a few months, but eventually we had to move on."

"They delivered results, but people found them difficult to work with."

None of these are automatically bad. They are just not enough for senior leadership.

At Senior Manager level, I want to hear how the person handled a lead or manager performance gap with explicit expectations, support, documentation, and team protection.

At Director level, I want to hear how they distinguished capability from role fit across managers, used peer and skip-level evidence, and changed scope before teams absorbed repeated misses.

At VP Engineering level, I want to hear how they handled a senior leader whose performance affected trust, succession, roadmap risk, and the leadership bar for the organization.

At CTO level, I want to hear how they separated recoverable gaps from trust-breaking behavior and acted before delay taught the company the wrong norm.

The most useful follow-ups are concrete:

- What expectation did they miss?
- What evidence would have changed your decision?
- What support or boundary did you provide before the final decision?
- Which alternative did you reject?
- What did waiting cost the team?
- How did you communicate the change without sharing private performance details?
- What mechanism changed afterward so the same situation would be caught earlier?

The strongest answers rarely sound like "I managed them out."

They sound more like:

"A well-liked manager in a 45-person product area was still operating through personal heroics. Three cross-team commitments slipped because planning, delegation, and lead ownership were not working. I made the role expectations explicit, gave a 60-day coaching plan with weekly support and evidence criteria, and tracked decision latency, planning quality, commitment misses, and lead ownership. When the same gaps remained, I moved them into a smaller role that fit their strengths. One roadmap area slowed during the transition, but skip-level confidence recovered, escalation volume dropped, and we changed promotion criteria so management scope required evidence of delegation, feedback, and conflict handling before expansion."

Senior underperformance management is not being nice, being tough, or outsourcing the hard part to HR.

It is clear expectations, fair evidence, timely action, team protection, and a better operating mechanism after the decision.
