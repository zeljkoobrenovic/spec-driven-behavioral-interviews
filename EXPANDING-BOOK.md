Yes. For **engineering managers** and **software engineers**, you should add different behavioral questions because the interview focus shifts.

For **senior technology leadership**, questions test strategy, organizational judgment, executive influence, and large-scale trade-offs.

For **engineering managers**, questions should test people leadership, delivery, coaching, team health, conflict, planning, and execution.

For **software engineers**, questions should test collaboration, ownership, learning, communication, technical judgment, handling feedback, and reliability in day-to-day work.

## Additional behavioral questions for Engineering Managers

### 1. Coaching and developing people

These are essential for engineering manager interviews.

**Questions to include:**

> Tell me about a time you helped an engineer grow significantly.

> Describe a time when someone on your team was struggling. What did you do?

> Tell me about a time you gave difficult feedback to an engineer.

> Describe a time when you helped a high-performing engineer reach the next level.

> Tell me about a time you had to adapt your management style for different people on your team.

**What good answers show:**
The manager understands individual motivation, gives clear feedback, follows up, creates growth opportunities, and does not confuse “being supportive” with avoiding hard conversations.

---

### 2. Managing underperformance

This is one of the most important areas for engineering managers.

**Questions:**

> Tell me about a time you had to manage an underperforming engineer.

> Describe a time when you waited too long to address a performance issue.

> Tell me about a time when someone disagreed with your feedback.

> Describe a situation where an engineer’s behavior hurt the team.

**What good answers show:**
Clarity, fairness, documentation, empathy, directness, and willingness to act.

**Weak signals:**
The candidate blames HR, avoids specifics, says “they eventually left,” or cannot explain what they personally did.

---

### 3. Team execution and delivery

Engineering managers are usually accountable for predictable delivery.

**Questions:**

> Tell me about a time your team missed a deadline.

> Describe a time you had to re-plan work because priorities changed.

> Tell me about a time you improved your team’s delivery process.

> Describe a time when your team committed to too much. What happened?

> Tell me about a time you had to balance speed and quality.

**What good answers show:**
Planning discipline, transparency, scope management, communication with stakeholders, and learning from delivery problems.

---

### 4. Handling conflict inside the team

Engineering managers need to resolve disagreement without suppressing it.

**Questions:**

> Tell me about a conflict between two engineers on your team.

> Describe a time when your team disagreed about a technical direction.

> Tell me about a time when you had to mediate between engineering and product.

> Describe a situation where team morale was low. What did you do?

**What good answers show:**
The manager listens, clarifies the real issue, sets expectations, separates people from the problem, and helps the team reach a decision.

---

### 5. Hiring and building the team

For engineering managers, hiring is often a core responsibility.

**Questions:**

> Tell me about a great hire you made. Why were they successful?

> Describe a hiring mistake you made.

> Tell me about a time you improved an interview or hiring process.

> Describe a time you had to build a diverse or complementary team.

> Tell me about a time you rejected a technically strong candidate.

**What good answers show:**
Thoughtful hiring criteria, awareness of team needs, ability to assess more than raw coding ability, and humility about hiring mistakes.

---

### 6. Stakeholder management

Engineering managers often sit between engineers, product managers, design, leadership, and customers.

**Questions:**

> Tell me about a time you had to push back on a product request.

> Describe a time you had to explain engineering constraints to non-engineers.

> Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager.

> Describe a time when stakeholders were unhappy with your team.

**What good answers show:**
Clear communication, business awareness, ability to say no constructively, and ability to protect the team without hiding behind the team.

---

### 7. Creating a healthy team culture

This is especially useful for your book because it distinguishes average managers from strong ones.

**Questions:**

> Tell me about a time you changed the culture of a team.

> Describe a time when your team lacked psychological safety.

> Tell me about a time you improved collaboration on your team.

> Describe a time when you had to rebuild trust.

**What good answers show:**
The manager notices team dynamics, changes rituals and incentives, models the desired behavior, and measures whether things improved.

---

## Additional behavioral questions for Software Engineers

For software engineers, the questions should be more concrete and closer to daily work.

### 1. Ownership

This is probably the most important behavioral category for engineers.

**Questions:**

> Tell me about a time you took ownership of a problem outside your assigned work.

> Describe a time when you found a bug, risk, or issue that others had missed.

> Tell me about a time you followed through on something difficult.

> Describe a time when you had to make sure a project succeeded despite obstacles.

**What good answers show:**
Initiative, reliability, persistence, and concern for outcomes rather than just assigned tasks.

---

### 2. Collaboration

Software engineering is team work. Behavioral interviews should test this directly.

**Questions:**

> Tell me about a time you worked closely with another engineer to solve a problem.

> Describe a time you helped a teammate.

> Tell me about a time you had a disagreement during code review.

> Describe a time when you had to work with product, design, data, or operations.

**What good answers show:**
Respectful communication, willingness to help, ability to disagree productively, and understanding of shared ownership.

---

### 3. Receiving and giving feedback

This is important for junior, mid-level, and senior engineers.

**Questions:**

> Tell me about a time you received difficult feedback.

> Describe a time when code review changed your approach.

> Tell me about a time you gave feedback to another engineer.

> Describe a time when you realized your first solution was wrong.

**What good answers show:**
Humility, learning mindset, technical maturity, and ability to improve without becoming defensive.

---

### 4. Learning and adaptability

For engineers, especially in fast-changing environments, this is critical.

**Questions:**

> Tell me about a time you had to learn a new technology quickly.

> Describe a time when you worked in an unfamiliar codebase.

> Tell me about a time when requirements changed late in the project.

> Describe a time when you had to change your opinion after learning something new.

**What good answers show:**
Curiosity, structured learning, flexibility, and the ability to become productive without needing perfect conditions.

---

### 5. Technical judgment

Behavioral questions can reveal technical judgment without becoming coding questions.

**Questions:**

> Tell me about a time you made a technical trade-off.

> Describe a time when you chose a simple solution over a more complex one.

> Tell me about a time you improved the quality of a system.

> Describe a time when you had to decide whether to refactor or ship.

> Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision.

**What good answers show:**
Pragmatism, understanding of trade-offs, ability to reason beyond personal preference, and concern for maintainability.

---

### 6. Handling production issues

This is useful for backend, infrastructure, platform, DevOps, SRE, and senior software engineering roles.

**Questions:**

> Tell me about a time you caused or helped resolve a production incident.

> Describe a time when you had to debug a difficult problem.

> Tell me about a time you improved observability, reliability, or performance.

> Describe a time when something failed after release.

**What good answers show:**
Calmness, accountability, systematic debugging, communication, and learning from incidents.

---

### 7. Communication

Many technically strong engineers fail because they cannot communicate clearly.

**Questions:**

> Tell me about a time you had to explain a technical problem to a non-technical person.

> Describe a time when written communication helped avoid confusion.

> Tell me about a time you had to document a complex system.

> Describe a time when poor communication caused a problem.

**What good answers show:**
Clarity, audience awareness, documentation habits, and ability to reduce ambiguity.

---

### 8. Prioritization and time management

This is especially important for senior engineers.

**Questions:**

> Tell me about a time you had too many things to do and had to prioritize.

> Describe a time when you pushed back on a request.

> Tell me about a time you had to balance urgent work with important long-term work.

> Describe a time when you chose not to do something.

**What good answers show:**
Focus, judgment, communication, and ability to manage trade-offs.

---

## Questions that work across all three roles

Some behavioral questions can appear in every chapter, but the expected answer should differ by level.

| Question                         | Software engineer answer should show | Engineering manager answer should show | Senior technology leader answer should show |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Tell me about a failure.         | Personal accountability and learning | Team/process improvement               | Organizational/systemic change              |
| Tell me about a conflict.        | Productive disagreement with peers   | Mediation and expectation setting      | Cross-functional or executive influence     |
| Tell me about ambiguity.         | Clarifying requirements              | Helping the team move forward          | Creating strategy from uncertainty          |
| Tell me about technical debt.    | Local code/system judgment           | Team prioritization                    | Business-risk framing                       |
| Tell me about a missed deadline. | Communication and ownership          | Planning and stakeholder management    | Portfolio-level trade-offs                  |
| Tell me about feedback.          | Learning and humility                | Coaching and performance management    | Leadership development and culture          |

## A useful distinction for your book

You can use this simple model:

**Software Engineer:**
“Can this person work well, learn, deliver, and make good technical decisions?”

**Senior Software Engineer / Tech Lead:**
“Can this person raise the quality and effectiveness of others without formal authority?”

**Engineering Manager:**
“Can this person build a healthy, effective team that delivers?”

**Director / VP / CTO:**
“Can this person shape the organization, strategy, culture, and business outcomes?”

That distinction will make the book much stronger because the same behavioral topic can be shown at different levels.
